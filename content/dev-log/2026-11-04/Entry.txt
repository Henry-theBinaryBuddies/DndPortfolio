Artifact 4 (CouchCrew):

Rebuilt the video call from scratch. The Jitsi version kept ending calls early and would not let us rejoin, and its public server wants a signed-in moderator, so a sign-in would only have helped whoever made the room. Adrian decided we should stop depending on other companies' video services (Daily wanted a card, Jitsi cut us off), so I wrote our own peer-to-peer WebRTC call.

How it works: when you press Join call, our Socket.IO server tells you who else is in the call, and your browser opens a direct connection to each of them. The server only passes the setup messages (who offers what, and the network addresses); the sound and video never go through it. Mic, camera and screen share all use the same connections. It needed three new message types on Adrian's server, which I flagged in the log.

I tested it with two browser tabs on my own computer. My testing browser blocks the camera, so I gave each tab a fake camera (a coloured box with a counter) and a fake tone. Both could see and hear each other, I could leave and rejoin, both could share a screen at the same time, and camera off, camera on and mute all showed up on the other side. The test also found a real bug: the second time anything changed on the call (like starting a screen share), the person who had joined threw an error and never answered, so the connection hung. The cause was that they re-attached their own tracks to a connection that already had them. A one-line flag fixed it and the same test passed.

What I still don't know: whether it works between real devices, on a phone, and on a school or phone-carrier network. A direct connection can be blocked there, and then a relay server is needed. I only configured free public servers that help browsers find each other, so some people may see "can't connect". Next is a real test with Adrian and John.
