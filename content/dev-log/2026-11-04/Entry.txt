Artifact 4 (CouchCrew):

Rebuilt the video call from scratch. The Jitsi version kept ending calls early and would not let us rejoin, and its public server wants a signed-in moderator, so a sign-in would only have helped whoever made the room. Adrian decided we should stop depending on other companies' video services (Daily wanted a card, Jitsi cut us off), so I wrote our own peer-to-peer WebRTC call.

How it works: when you press Join call, our Socket.IO server tells you who else is in the call, and your browser opens a direct connection to each of them. The server only passes the setup messages (who offers what, and the network addresses); the sound and video never go through it. Mic, camera and screen share all use the same connections. It needed three new message types on Adrian's server, which I flagged in the log.

I tested it with two browser tabs on my own computer. My testing browser blocks the camera, so I gave each tab a fake camera (a coloured box with a counter) and a fake tone. Both could see and hear each other, I could leave and rejoin, both could share a screen at the same time, and camera off, camera on and mute all showed up on the other side. The test also found a real bug: the second time anything changed on the call (like starting a screen share), the person who had joined threw an error and never answered, so the connection hung. The cause was that they re-attached their own tracks to a connection that already had them. A one-line flag fixed it and the same test passed.

What I still don't know: whether it works between real devices, on a phone, and on a school or phone-carrier network. A direct connection can be blocked there, and then a relay server is needed. I only configured free public servers that help browsers find each other, so some people may see "can't connect". Next is a real test with Adrian and John.

Later the same day I tested the live call on two networks: my laptop on one and my phone on another. Each could see only its own camera, and the tile for the other person said "can't connect". I took that for the usual problem: with only free public servers to help browsers find each other, two devices on different networks often cannot connect directly. So I added a relay server: our server asks Cloudflare's TURN service for short-lived credentials when you join a call, so the secret key stays on the server. That checked out locally (the server got real relay credentials back). (Correction, Nov 6: the failure was actually a firewall on my work network that blocks video calls, so the relay was not what that test needed. It is still a reasonable safety net, but I have not shown it fixing anything.)

I also worked out what the relay would cost. Cloudflare gives 1,000 GB a month free and then charges one cent a GB. My estimate, at about 1 megabit per second per camera, is that a fully relayed two-person call uses about 1 GB an hour, three people about 2.7 GB, and ten people about 40 GB, which would use the free allowance in around 25 hours. Only calls that cannot connect directly use the relay, so a class project should stay well under, and I set billing alerts anyway. The bigger limit for large groups is not cost but upload: in this design everyone sends their video to everyone else, so ten people would each need around 9 megabits per second of upload, more than many home connections or phones can manage. Past four or five people the call will stutter however much we pay. Fixing that properly needs a server that forwards video, which is out of scope for Nov 8.

I also noticed the shared YouTube video is slightly out of sync between my two devices. That is the watch player (John's job), so I passed it on.

Side Quest (Effigy Engine):

The Dragonborn. The humanoid head cannot be stretched into a dragon's, so I followed the plan Codex wrote and sculpted it separately. A Blender script builds the bust headless: the head and neck are a signed distance field (muzzle, jaw, brow plates, sunk eyes, nostrils, horns, cheek spines and a crest), joined to the human chest and remeshed at 1 mm. Then shingled scales, throat scutes and horn rings are cut in. The test STL it writes is checked as one watertight solid.

In the app it is a family of its own at the end of the Race list. Its 11 MB model only loads the first time it is chosen, and if it fails to load, the bust goes back to the humanoid and says why. It has its own sliders (muzzle length and width, jaw, brow plates, neck, horn size and sweep, spines), each a smooth warp of the sculpt, with tests that no setting folds the surface. Its paint comes from the cells the scales were cut from, so each scale is a slightly different shade, with a paler underside and slit-pupil eyes. Over the day the muzzle got shorter and deeper and the scales were sorted into large plates over the crown and cheeks and small scales down the muzzle, laid in rows. That fixed the look of round cobbles.

Codex also did a pass on the humanoid forms: human facial planes, a designed swept hairline, broader beard masses and long-hair tresses. I reviewed it and fixed a few things, such as a dark hair edge that brought back the hard, stepped hairline.

One fix for tabletop size: the Dragonborn's long muzzle ran off one side of the one inch square while the other side had room to spare. Busts are now centred over the base by the middle of their whole outline seen from above, which took the Dragonborn from 23 mm tall to 29 mm. A muzzle 1.5 cm shorter that afternoon brought it to 32 mm.

Separately, I found out my Ender 3 most likely has the stock 0.4 mm nozzle, not the 0.2 mm the print sizes assume. I put printing on hold to keep building.
