Side Quest (Scenic Sorcerer):

The map maker needed art, and that took most of the day. Claude first found a hand-inked set of free dungeon pictures and had it ready to add, but I had not seen it, and when I did I said it looked awful and had it all removed. Claude then drew a small set of its own, and then a softer version, and I said those looked flat and basic and that it should stop making its own art. So we went looking for art I could use properly. The free pictures with open licences were nearly all small pixel art, and the good painted ones came with rules about where they could be used. Most forbid putting them inside a program that lets people place them, which is exactly what this is. Tom Cartos's Open Map License is the exception: it allows his props inside free map software, as long as they are never sold or paywalled, and asks for a credit. Claude checked the licence text before we chose it. I downloaded his bundle myself, and Claude shrank the pictures so they load quickly and saved them as WebP, which I asked him about and he said was fine.

Claude first added 143 of his 800 props so I could judge them, then, when I liked them, most of the rest: 676 props in 14 kinds, such as furniture, containers, dungeon, nature, weapons, alchemy and farm. Pipes, cogs, sails and cliffs were left out, and so were extra copies of near-identical pieces. They are placed at their true size on the grid, and they are not tinted, because his licence does not allow editing the art. His name and a link to his licence are on the page and in the README. The 563 MB of originals are kept out of the project.

Then the things that make it easier to use. Ctrl+C, Ctrl+X, Ctrl+V and Ctrl+D copy, cut, paste and duplicate. Holding Alt while dragging drags a copy. Right-clicking a thing opens a menu to move it up or down a layer, copy, flip or delete it, and right-clicking empty space pastes there. There are shortcuts for everything else too, and a list that opens with the question mark. I also asked for eight more floors, and there are now snow, ice, mossy, rocky, forest, magical, lava and metal beside the old ones, all painted by code.

Then stairs. The art bundle had none, and the free stair packs I could find were 3D models, so Claude made the editor draw them: stone, wood, metal, rock, moss, ice, snow and dirt, narrow or wide, going up or down. I then asked for spiral staircases, which wind round a post with a curved arrow, and for the stairs to get shaded as they climb so they look like they have height. Claude found the first version had its shadow on the wrong side of each step, which made them look flat, and fixed that. A selected staircase can have its number of steps, size, material and direction changed, and stairs move, turn, copy and save like any other stamp.

Everything was checked in a real browser, with 169 tests passing. I decided it is good enough for the prototype. It still needs putting online, and I have not tried its tabletop files in a real tabletop.
